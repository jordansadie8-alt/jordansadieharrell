## Definition  
User interface design choices that are carefully crafted to trick users into doing things they did not mean to do, such as buying insurance, signing up for recurring bills, or sharing more data than intended. It is the weaponization of cognitive biases against the user.

Coined by Harry Brignull, "Dark Patterns" are not accidental bad design; they are precise psychological attacks. They exploit known heuristics, like the fear of missing out (scarcity), the tendency to skim text (misdirection), or the difficulty of finding hidden links (obstruction). In the digital economy, they represent short-term profit extraction at the expense of long-term brand trust. If *Trustworthy UX* is "service," *Dark Patterns* are "theft."

## Signs / markers
- **Confirmshaming:** When the option to decline is worded to insult you (e.g., "No, I prefer to bleed money" instead of just "No thanks").
- **The Roach Motel:** A situation where it is easy to get in (One-click signup) but almost impossible to get out (Call center required to cancel).
- **Forced Continuity:** When a "free trial" silently converts into a paid subscription without a reminder or easy cancellation link.

## Examples (culture / tech / daily life)
- **Airline Checkout Flows:** The classic "opt-out" insurance box that is hidden at the bottom of the page or worded in a double negative ("Check this box if you do NOT want to not be insured").
- **Cookie Banners:** Making the "Accept All" button bright green and massive, while the "Reject All" button is a tiny, grey text link buried in a sub-menu.
- **"Only 2 rooms left!"** Travel sites using fake urgency counters to panic the user into booking immediately, even when inventory is high.
