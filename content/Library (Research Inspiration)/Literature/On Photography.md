# On Photography

## Why I saved this
- To understand the cultural and social implications of photography as a mass medium.
- To explore critical perspectives on how images shape perception, reality, and history.
- To examine the ethical dilemmas surrounding photographic representation, voyeurism, and desensitization.

## Key themes
- **The predatory nature of photography:** Sontag argues that taking a picture is an act of aggression and appropriation, violating the subject's integrity.
- **Photography as an acquisitive activity:** Photography allows people to collect the world, turning experiences and environments into consumable objects.
- **The aestheticizing of suffering:** Photographs of pain and tragedy can beautify and distance the viewer from the reality of the event, potentially leading to moral anesthesia.
- **The democratization of vision:** Photography makes everything potential subject matter, leveling distinctions between the beautiful and the ugly, the significant and the trivial.
- **Reality vs. Image:** The proliferation of photographic images alters our relationship with reality, often making the image seem more real or significant than the experience itself.
- **Photography and time/mortality:** Photographs are memento mori, freezing moments in time and emphasizing the transience of life.

## Key quotes
- "To collect photographs is to collect the world."
- "To photograph is to appropriate the thing photographed. It means putting oneself into a certain relation to the world that feels like knowledge—and, therefore, like power."
- "Photographs furnish evidence. Something we hear about, but doubt, seems proven when we’re shown a photograph of it."
- "To photograph people is to violate them, by seeing them as they never see themselves, by having knowledge of them they can never have; it turns people into objects that can be symbolically possessed."
- "All photographs are memento mori. To take a photograph is to participate in another person’s (or thing’s) mortality, vulnerability, mutability."
- "The camera makes reality atomic, manageable, and opaque. It is a view of the world which denies interconnectedness, continuity, but which confers on each moment the character of a mystery."

## Notes / takeaways
- **In Plato's Cave:** Photography creates a new visual code and ethics of seeing. It turns the world into an anthology of images and fundamentally alters our perception of reality.
- **America, Seen Through Photographs, Darkly:** Discusses the democratization of beauty through photography (referencing Whitman) and how photographers like Diane Arbus shifted focus to the trivial, the ugly, and the freakish, reflecting a darker view of America.
- **Melancholy Objects:** Explores photography as a surrealist art form that finds beauty in the mundane, the decayed, and the discarded. It creates a nostalgic and fragmentary relationship with the past.
- **The Heroism of Vision:** Examines how photography has changed the standards of seeing, promoting an "abstracting eye" that finds formal beauty in unexpected places, influenced by and influencing modernist painting.
- **Photographic Evangels:** Analyzes the various and often contradictory claims photographers make about their medium (realism vs. expressionism, science vs. art) and its relationship to truth and beauty.
- **The Image-World:** Argues that reality is increasingly interpreted through images, leading to a society where images have more authority than lived experience, and consumption of images becomes a primary activity.
